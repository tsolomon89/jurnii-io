# The Europe iGaming Market Report, H1 2026

*The rising compliance floor and the journey execution that separates operators above it.*

---

## Executive summary

Europe is the most regulatorily mature and most fragmented iGaming region in the world. It is not one market. It is a set of national markets, each with its own framework, its own identity system, its own payment rail, and its own competitive floor. What they share is a direction of travel: regulators have made a large part of the experience mandatory, from identity verification to deposit limits to self-exclusion registers. That has raised the compliance floor for everyone, and pushed the competitive contest to the layer above it.

Six findings define the half-year across the six markets studied: the Netherlands, France, Italy, Finland, Greece, and Norway.

1. **There is no single European market.** Feature coverage, journey scores, and competitive structure differ market by market, and the same multi-market brand scores differently in each. Compliance can be centralised. Execution cannot.
2. **You can lead a market and still be "Poor".** In France, the leading operator scores 57 out of 100 on the Jurnii composite, a "Poor"-band score, first in a field where every operator is under-performing. The Netherlands, benchmarked separately in August 2026, returns the same verdict on a different field: the leader reaches 59 and no licensed Dutch operator clears the Poor band at all. Market leadership in Europe is currently a relative position inside a weak field, not evidence of a strong experience.
3. **Digital identity is the onboarding moat.** Where a market offers a national digital-identity path, the operators that integrate it activate accounts in minutes, and the operators that do not add days to first withdrawal. Same regulation, opposite outcomes.
4. **Payment breadth is not converging.** The same brand fields three payment methods in one market and fifteen in another. In a market where every operator has cleared the same compliance bar, the cashier is one of the few surfaces that still visibly separates them.
5. **Responsible gambling has become a design surface.** Every compliant operator meets the mandatory bar. What separates them is placement: in-flow reassurance versus a footer link, and whether the strongest compliance posture is undermined by the weakest help journey.
6. **The differentiation has moved upstairs, and most operators are not competing there deliberately.** The strong scores across the region are on front doors. The weak scores are on the deposit, the cashier, casino discovery, and getting help, the surfaces regulation does not prescribe.

The through-line is that regulatory maturity is a levelling force. It closes the gap on the basics and moves differentiation to the journey execution regulators do not specify. In Europe, that is where share is now won and lost, one national market at a time.

## 1. Market context: a rising floor across a fragmented region

Europe reaches iGaming regulatory maturity first, and the maturity takes a different legal form in every market. The Netherlands operates a licensed market under the Kansspelautoriteit, with a national self-exclusion register and a citizen-identity verification requirement. Italy licenses under ADM, with national digital-identity schemes built into the onboarding path. France licenses online sports betting and poker under the ANJ, while online casino remains outside the regulated perimeter, which shapes the entire product mix. Greece licenses under the Hellenic Gaming Commission. Finland operates a state monopoly moving toward a licensing model, with dot-com-facing operators competing for Finnish players ahead of reform. Norway retains a monopoly, with offshore-facing brands operating around it.

Six markets, six frameworks. What they have in common is that the regulator has made a large part of the experience mandatory. Identity verification, mandatory deposit and time limits, self-exclusion registers, and responsible-gambling messaging are no longer differentiators. They are enforced table stakes.

That has a commercial consequence operators consistently under-read. When the regulator standardises the basics, the basics stop separating operators. Two licensed brands in the same market now clear the same compliance bar in similar ways. The differences that decide share sit in the parts of the journey the regulator does not specify: how fast onboarding actually completes, how broad the payment set is, how well the casino lobby helps a player find a game, and whether help is reachable at all. Regulatory maturity has moved the battleground one floor up. Below the floor, everyone complies. Above it, the market is open, and the data shows most operators are not competing there deliberately.

The same structural conditions that define the UK apply across the region. Platform commoditisation means many operators run on shared B2B cores and compete on presentation rather than engine. Player multi-homing means the same fragile loyalty and the same instant switching. Rising acquisition costs mean the payback on a hard-won registration depends on the journey that follows it. In Europe, those conditions meet a rising compliance floor, and the combination pushes the entire contest upstairs.

The clearest evidence that Europe is not one market is that operators do not carry their position across borders. Feature coverage, the share of audited features an operator ships, varies widely market to market, and the leaders differ in each.

The fields are tight and the leaders rotate. In the Netherlands, nine points separate first from fifth. In Italy, a seven-brand field is compressed inside eleven points. A multi-market operator can lead Finland and sit last in Italy in the same quarter, on the same brand, because the execution required to lead is set by each local competitive floor, not by a regional standard.

The French market is measured on the Jurnii UX composite, a 0-to-100 score across four commercially weighted dimensions, and it produces the region's defining paradox.

The leading operator scores 57 and leads a field where every operator sits in the "Poor" band. Beneath the headline, the pattern is systemic: 41% of the recommendations logged against the market leader are high-severity, nearly twice the cross-market average, concentrated in the journeys that convert. Leading a poor-performing market is a commercial risk disguised as a competitive advantage. A number-one position measured only against local rivals hides the fact that the entire local experience sits below the standard the best operators set elsewhere. In a mature market, that gap is a liability waiting for a better-executed entrant.

The surfaces the regulator does not standardise are where European operators separate, and the region shows the same pattern in every market: strong front doors, weak conversion and support journeys.

### Digital identity is the onboarding moat

Every European market mandates KYC. What separates operators is how they satisfy it, and the national digital-identity systems are the single biggest lever on registration friction.

In Italy, the SPID and CIE digital-identity schemes let an operator verify a new account in real time, in minutes, with no manual document upload. In the same market, a major operator running a classic, manual-only flow takes eight to ten minutes to register and adds up to three days before a first withdrawal can clear. Same regulation, same market, opposite outcomes.

The Nordics show the same split. In Finland, one operator auto-verifies identity at deposit through open banking, and another accepts national bank credentials as an alternative to document upload, while a third offering neither runs the slowest onboarding-to-cashout path in the field. In the Netherlands, a bank-identity fast path collapses registration to a single click, against a manual continuous-scroll form of roughly thirteen field groups at a rival. The regulation is not the differentiator. The integration of the regulation into the journey is, and the operators still running classic manual flows in markets that offer a real-time alternative are conceding the first and most fragile moment of the relationship.

If regulation levelled the compliance floor, it did nothing to level payments, and the spread across European markets is enormous even holding the operator constant.

The same brand fields three methods in Italy and fifteen in Greece. Payment breadth maps directly to the deposit journey score, and the deposit journey is where money enters the business. A thin cashier in a mature market is not a cost-saving. It is a conversion tax at the highest-value step, paid every time a player's preferred method is missing and the deposit is abandoned. In markets where every operator has cleared the same compliance bar, the cashier is one of the few surfaces left that visibly separates them, and it is being under-built.

### Responsible gambling is a placement decision

European responsible-gambling regulation is strict and getting stricter. Every compliant operator meets the bar. What separates them is where they put the tooling and whether it reads as reassurance or obligation. In the Netherlands, one operator surfaces a responsible-gambling block inside the registration flow, the only in-flow placement in its cohort, where every rival relegates it to a footer, and it surfaces verification eligibility before a withdrawal amount is even entered, turning a compliance step into an expectation-setting one. In other markets, the strongest posture belongs to whichever operator makes limit-setting a mandatory step in the flow. The signal is that responsible gambling has become a trust-and-conversion surface, not only a compliance obligation, and the standard is being set market by market by whichever operator moves first.

But compliance strength does not compensate for a weak journey elsewhere, because the player experiences both. In the Dutch market, the operator with the strongest compliance posture, a six-hour daily time-limit cap where rivals allow eight and a hard deposit gate behind verification, also holds the market's lowest score on getting help: no documented complaints or alternative-dispute-resolution route, help off the persistent header, and only partial local-language support. The floor is mandatory. The execution above it, where limits, help, and transparency become trust rather than friction, is where the differentiation actually sits.

### Casino discovery and help are the common gaps

Across the region, the recurring weaknesses are the same. Casino lobbies ship without provider, RTP, volatility, or theme filters, and often without demo play. Promotional terms and wagering requirements sit buried behind a call to action. Help journeys lack a visible support route, a complaints procedure, or a 24-hour availability indicator. In the French market specifically, the highest-severity findings cluster on exactly these surfaces: no visible search in the sportsbook, deposit-limit setting absent from the flow, fee transparency missing at deposit, and contact options buried in the help section. These are the journeys regulation does not prescribe and most operators under-invest in, and they are where retention is decided after acquisition is paid for.

## 4. Market-by-market notes

**Netherlands.** A licensed market with a strong compliance culture. The leading brands sit close together on feature coverage, and the market's strengths are concentrated in verification, withdrawals, and responsible-gambling posture. Its weaknesses are concentrated in getting help, casino discovery, and account transparency, with one audit logging 136 recommendations against a single operator and page-performance debt severe enough to register Time to Interactive around 25 seconds. The iDIN bank-identity path is the market's clearest onboarding differentiator, and the operators without it carry the region's heaviest manual registration forms.

**France.** The defining feature is regulatory scope: online casino sits outside the regulated perimeter, so the licensed market is a sports-betting and poker contest. The market leader's 57 composite, first in a "Poor"-band field, is the region's clearest illustration that a local number-one ranking is not evidence of a strong experience. The high-severity findings are systemic and concentrated on conversion journeys, which makes the market both under-served and exposed to a better-executed entrant.

**Italy.** The market where digital identity most clearly separates operators. SPID and CIE enable real-time verification, and the operators that integrate them activate in minutes while classic-only flows add days to first withdrawal. Italy also runs the region's widest payment-breadth spread, from three methods to seventeen, which makes the cashier a live competitive surface. Feature coverage is compressed across a seven-brand field, so execution on onboarding and payments is the separator.

**Finland and Norway.** The Nordic markets sit in regulatory transition and monopoly respectively, with dot-com-facing operators competing around the state incumbents. The competitive signal is instant onboarding: open-banking verification through Trustly-style flows and national bank-identity acceptance are the fast paths, and the operators without them run the slowest onboarding-to-cashout journeys. Registration UX is where these brands compete most visibly; one Norwegian-facing brand's registration re-audit moved from 65 to 68 after a redesign that fixed input controls but left the core conversion blockers, including password guidance and error recovery, untouched, which is a common pattern across the region: incremental fixes that do not lift a brand out of its rating band.

**Greece.** A licensed market where the competitive separator is payment breadth, running as high as fifteen methods, and where the responsible-gambling gap is most visible: onboarding flows that carry no in-flow responsible-gambling reference while rivals make limit-setting mandatory. Feature coverage is led by the international entrants, with the strongest local execution close behind.

## 5. Structural and corporate dynamics

Two structural facts shape commercial strategy across the region.

**Compliance centralises; execution does not.** The multi-market groups can run a single compliance function across borders, but the data shows they cannot run a single experience. The same brand fields three payment methods in one European market and fifteen in another, activates in minutes in one and days in another, and carries a strong in-flow responsible-gambling placement in one market and a footer link in the next. Those are journey decisions made market by market, and they are where European share is won and lost. A group optimising for a regional average optimises for none of its markets.

**Capital and consolidation concentrate globally.** The corporate backdrop is one of tier-one gaming capital consolidating on Wall Street, with the largest European-heritage groups holding their primary listings in New York. That concentration funds the multi-year investment required to compete on execution across a fragmented region, and it raises the premium on defensible, evidence-based commercial positioning. In a region where a market leader can sit in the "Poor" band, the operators who can prove their position against the best execution anywhere, rather than the best of a local field, are the ones best placed as the region consolidates.

Regulatory reform is the wildcard. Markets moving from monopoly toward licensing will open competitive fields that do not exist today, and the operators who enter them with a benchmarked view of what "good" looks like across the region will set the standard the incumbents have not had to meet.

## 6. Outlook: what to watch in H2 2026

**Differentiation stays upstairs, and widens.** As the compliance floor continues to rise, the basics will separate operators even less, and the surfaces above the floor, onboarding speed, payment breadth, casino discovery, and help, will separate them even more. The operators who invest there deliberately will pull away from the ones treating compliance as the finish line.

**Digital identity becomes table stakes for onboarding.** The gap between a real-time digital-identity flow and a classic manual one is measured in days to first withdrawal, and it is the clearest, most fixable competitive gap in the region. Expect the operators still running manual flows in markets that offer a real-time path to close it, and expect the ones that move first to hold an onboarding advantage in the interim.

**The "Poor-band leader" is exposed.** A local number-one ranking measured against an under-performing field is a signal, not a moat. The markets where the whole field sits in the "Poor" band are the markets most exposed to a better-executed entrant, and France is the clearest example.

**Reform reshapes the competitive set.** Markets in transition toward licensing will change shape, and the incumbents that competed against a monopoly will suddenly compete against operators benchmarked to the best execution across Europe. The visibility to know where "good" sits, market by market, is the entry advantage.

The compliance floor in Europe is rising, and it will keep rising. That is not the threat. The threat is treating the floor as the finish line, when the contest has already moved upstairs. The operators who win mature markets are the ones benchmarking themselves against the best execution anywhere, not the best of a local field that may all be under-performing together.
