# From UX Audit to Backlog: How Operators Should Prioritise Recommendations

By Tristan Dunk, CPO

In the highly competitive iGaming industry, user experience has become the primary driver of customer retention and brand differentiation. As a result, operators frequently invest in external UX audits to evaluate their digital platforms. These audits typically culminate in the delivery of a massive, 150-page PDF report filled with screenshots, heuristic evaluations, and design recommendations.
Unfortunately, most of these audits fail to deliver commercial value. They sit in shared folders and function as digital gravestones. They are static, overwhelming, and completely detached from the product team's engineering workflows. A Chief Product Officer (CPO) faces a backlog that is already congested with compliance updates, security patches, and platform integrations. Delivering a long list of design suggestions without a prioritization framework only adds noise to an over-allocated roadmap. To turn UX audits into live product improvements, operators must adopt a methodology that translates design observations into structured, commercial priorities.
Furthermore, this delayed data cycle creates friction across internal departments. CRM teams, trading desks, marketing managers, and product directors all require competitive context to execute their roles. When these departments receive out-of-date, weekly PDF reports, they are forced to make tactical decisions based on historical patterns rather than current market conditions. The resulting lack of alignment often leads to mismatched campaigns, where CRM teams launch promotions that are immediately undercut by a competitor's live offer.

## The Four Dimensions of Jurnii UX

To build an actionable backlog, design evaluations must be categorized into structured dimensions that reflect the entire user journey. Jurnii UX evaluates platforms across four distinct dimensions, establishing a common vocabulary for product, design, and engineering teams.
The first dimension is Journey Effectiveness. This focuses on the conversion funnel, tracking the player's progression through registration, KYC verification, deposit, search, and withdrawal. Friction points in these core flows have the most immediate, negative impact on Net Gaming Revenue (NGR) and first-time deposits (FTDs). For example, a registration form that fails to save inputs when a player is prompted to verify their SMS code represents a major journey blocker. Players forced to re-enter their details will abandon the journey, directly hurting acquisition. Journey effectiveness represents the highest priority in the UX backlog.
The second dimension is Usability. This evaluates the clarity and layout of the interface, ensuring that players can navigate the sports lobby, compile bet builders, or find casino games without cognitive overload. An example of a usability friction point is a bet builder that disables the main CTA when conflicting options are selected, without displaying a message explaining the conflict. This leaves the user confused and unable to resolve the issue. Usability issues must be prioritized based on how frequently they disrupt player actions.
The third dimension is Performance. This measures technical speed, device responsiveness, and Core Web Vitals across different networks. Technical performance directly controls conversion rates; Jurnii’s data shows that a 1.2-second delay on a deposit screen correlates with transaction abandonment. In slot libraries, slow asset loading can lead to high Layout Shifts, causing users to misclick and launch the wrong game. Performance metrics provide developers with objective, non-negotiable targets.
The fourth dimension is Perception. This evaluates visual design, brand credibility, and aesthetic trust. A dated, cluttered, or inconsistent interface erodes brand trust, causing players to question the security of their deposits. An example of a perception issue is when the cashier page uses a different logo, color scheme, or layout from the main sportsbook landing page, triggering anxiety about the platform's legitimacy. Perception issues are often dismissed as subjective design opinions, but when structured within Jurnii's framework, they are recognized as critical factors in customer lifetime value.

## Ridding the Backlog of Subjective Bias

The biggest obstacle to UX implementation is subjective opinion. Product roadmaps are frequently derailed by internal political debates over visual preferences. A designer may advocate for a modern aesthetic, while a trading director demands more promo banners, and a marketing lead prioritizes acquisition hooks.
To resolve these conflicts, product managers must replace subjective design debates with objective, commercial severity scoring. Jurnii UX ranks over 70 recommendations using a standardized methodology that calculates the revenue impact of each friction point.
The commercial severity score is calculated based on three variables: funnel depth, player frequency, and implementation effort. Funnel depth measures how close the friction point is to the transaction; a problem on the deposit screen is weighted higher than one on the sports blog. Player frequency estimates how many users experience the issue. Implementation effort determines the engineering resources required for the fix, evaluating whether a change resides in the front-end presentation layer or requires complex backend platform work.
By combining these variables, product teams can calculate a standardized commercial score for every recommendation. This score determines the issue's position in the backlog, aligning product, engineering, and commercial stakeholders around a single, data-backed roadmap. Junior product managers no longer need to defend design decisions; the data establishes the priority.

## Managing Third-Party Vendor and Platform Constraints

iGaming CPOs operate within severe technical constraints. The vast majority of sportsbooks and casinos rely on third-party Player Account Management (PAM) systems and sportsbook feeds. These B2B platform providers control the core databases and transactional APIs, leaving operators with limited front-end flexibility.
When an audit recommends a complex change to the registration or KYC verification flow, the product team is often told by their B2B platform vendor that the change will take six months and cost thousands of pounds. This constraint is where many UX audits stall.
A structured UX backlog solves this by categorizing recommendations based on where the implementation occurs. We separate fixes into client-side styling changes (which the operator’s front-end team can implement independently) and platform-level database modifications (which require vendor cooperation).
By focusing on client-side styling improvements—such as improving field validation error messaging, optimizing button placement, or clarifying input guidelines—operators can implement immediate, high-impact UX fixes without waiting for vendor roadmap slots. At the same time, the commercial severity scores provide the product team with the evidence needed to build a business case for the platform vendor, accelerating the delivery of complex database changes.

## Transforming Audits Into Continuous Product Improvement

An audit is a point-in-time snapshot. In the high-velocity iGaming market, where competitors launch new features weekly, a static audit becomes obsolete within months. To maintain a competitive product advantage, operators must move past static PDF reviews and establish a continuous, data-driven prioritization framework.
