# What Operators Miss Between Manual Competitor Reviews

By Mitch Vidler, CCO

In the high-velocity iGaming market, competitive intelligence is often treated as a periodic compliance exercise or a static reporting task rather than an active commercial radar. Most sportsbooks and casinos rely on manual competitor reviews to track the market. Typically, a junior analyst opens a list of competitor websites on a Monday morning, takes screenshots of homepage banners, logs signup offers in a spreadsheet, and compiles a PDF report.
This manual auditing process is a commercial liability. In a market where competitive dynamics change by the hour, a weekly or monthly review is a historical archive, not a commercial radar. By the time a manual report is compiled, approved, and circulated, the competitor has already updated their homepage creative, adjusted their odds boosts, or changed their stakes limits. Jurnii’s market data shows that 70% to 80% of UK players hold 3 to 5 active accounts simultaneously, switching between apps in seconds to capture the highest value. In this environment, operating with stale competitive data leaves you blind to the immediate pricing moves that control player acquisition.
Furthermore, this delayed data cycle creates friction across internal departments. CRM teams, trading desks, marketing managers, and product directors all require competitive context to execute their roles. When these departments receive out-of-date, weekly PDF reports, they are forced to make tactical decisions based on historical patterns rather than current market conditions. The resulting lack of alignment often leads to mismatched campaigns, where CRM teams launch promotions that are immediately undercut by a competitor's live offer.

## The Three Blind Spots of Manual Auditing

Relying on manual sweeps creates three distinct blind spots that distort an operator’s view of the market.
First, Dynamic Boost Timings. Sportsbooks frequently launch odds boosts only hours before kick-off to capture late-money volume. These boosts are highly volatile and are often adjusted dynamically based on liability. A manual sweep conducted at 9:00 AM on a Monday will completely miss the pre-match pricing wars that occur at 6:00 PM on a Tuesday. Operators who rely on manual reviews are unaware of when competitors are actively conceding margin to capture peak player traffic.
Second, Overnight and Off-Peak Propositions. Competitors often run short-lived campaigns during off-peak hours—such as overnight boosts for US sports or early morning racing enhancements—to target specific player segments. These promotions are designed to disappear before standard working hours, making them invisible to manual audits. By ignoring these off-peak windows, commercial teams fail to understand the competitor's true promotional volume and cohort acquisition strategy.
Third, Competitive Velocity and Reaction Patterns. If a major operator launches an aggressive price boost on an England match, their rivals will often react within minutes by launching a matching boost or a counter-promotion. Manual audits capture static snapshots of the market, making it impossible to analyze these response loops. Without measuring competitive velocity, you cannot identify which competitor is the pricing pioneer driving the market and which competitor is the copycat reacting to shifts.
Additionally, manual sweeps fail to account for the complexity of modern promotional structures. Calculating the actual generosity of an odds boost requires more than just noting the fractional prices. It demands an understanding of stake limits, payout terms, and combination requirements. A competitor offering a massive headline boost may apply a highly restrictive five-pound stake limit, while another operator offers a smaller boost with unlimited stakes. A manual spreadsheet that only records the headline price will misrepresent the true competitiveness of these offers, leading to flawed positioning.

## The Operational and Strategic Cost of Manual Research

Beyond the strategic blind spots, manual competitive tracking represents a massive operational drain. Commercial and marketing teams waste dozens of hours every week manually checking competitor sites, logging into dummy customer accounts, and copying promo codes.
Jurnii’s database shows that the average operator spends over 30 hours per week per commercial team on manual data collection. This manual research is highly inefficient and prone to human error. Analysts frequently miss promotion terms, miscalculate boosted margins, or fail to record short-lived offers.
The repetitive nature of manual tracking also contributes to organizational inefficiency. Junior analysts spend their time copying data from websites instead of performing high-value statistical analysis. This leads to high employee turnover and a lack of analytical maturity within the commercial team. Instead of interpreting market trends and identifying growth opportunities, analysts are reduced to data collectors.
Furthermore, the output of manual tracking is highly subjective. A spreadsheet compiled by one analyst will categorize a promotion differently from one compiled by another, leading to inconsistent baseline data. When commercial directors make strategic decisions based on subjective, incomplete spreadsheets, they risk launching copycat promotions that dilute margins without driving incremental NGR.
