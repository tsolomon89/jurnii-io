# There Is No LATAM Playbook

### Operators keep treating Latin America as one market. The data says it is at least three. The proof is that the same operator, running the same brand, scores completely differently in Brazil, Peru, and Argentina, and gives ground on a different journey in each.

---

## The argument in one line

"LATAM" is a travel region, not a commercial one. Brazil, Peru, and Argentina reward different journeys, different payment behaviour, and different onboarding, and a single regional playbook underperforms the local execution in every one of them.

---

## Why now

The temptation to treat Latin America as a single expansion market is understandable and expensive. The markets share a continent, two dominant languages, and a wave of regulation. They do not share a player, a payment habit, an identity system, or a competitive structure.

The cleanest way to prove this is to hold the operator constant and change the market. When the same brand, with the same product philosophy, the same trading approach, and the same design language, is audited across Brazil, Peru, and Argentina, its scores do not travel. Each market leads on a different journey. Each exposes a different gap. That is not an operator inconsistency. It is the markets themselves being different, and the audit is simply reading the difference.

Jurnii's multi-market feature audit covers 31 brands across nine journeys, roughly 200 feature checks per market. What follows is what the region looks like when you stop averaging it.

---

## Evidence 1: The same operator leads a different journey in every market

Hold one multi-market operator constant and read its journey coverage across the three markets. The shape changes completely.

| Journey | Peru | Brazil | Argentina |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registration | 78 | 69 | 67 |
| Account access | 71 | 63 | 38 |
| Sportsbook | 86 | 89 | 80 |
| Payments | 90 | 50 | 75 |
| Casino | 67 | 39 | 55 |
| Bonuses | 70 | 60 | 75 |
| Help | 67 | 92 | 64 |

Same brand. Same quarter. Payments coverage runs from 90 in Peru to 50 in Brazil. Casino from 67 to 39. Help from 92 to 64. This is not a brand that is "good" or "bad" at experience. It is a brand meeting three markets with different expectations, different rails, and different competitive floors, and the execution required to lead is different in each.

The competitive context around it moves just as much. In Peru, this operator ranks first of five on feature coverage. In Brazil, it sits mid-pack behind the scale players. In Argentina, it edges a four-brand field by two points. A regional strategy optimised for the Peru position is wrong for Brazil and irrelevant in Argentina. There is no single "win LATAM" configuration, because there is no single LATAM.

---

## Evidence 2: Onboarding is a different problem in each market

The registration journey is where the markets diverge most visibly, because each is built on a different national identity system and a different friction tolerance.

**Brazil** runs on the CPF. The winning pattern surfaces it first and pre-fills the rest of the form from it, and the market punishes operators that bolt heavy verification onto the front of the flow. In the same cohort, effective steps to activation run from a single-screen form to a three-step flow plus mandatory facial recognition. That biometric step is the highest onboarding friction in the market, imposed at the highest-intent moment.

**Peru** offers a national-document fast path: one operator's DNI route completes registration in a single step, against a three-to-four-step manual flow elsewhere. The lever here is the fast path, not the form.

**Argentina** is built on DNI plus CUIT, with the strongest brands auto-filling both, adding a live password-strength meter, and carrying a sworn age and politically-exposed-person declaration inside the flow. The Argentine market also verifies identity against the national registry at submission, a structural step that does not exist the same way north of the border.

Three markets, three onboarding problems. An operator that ships one registration flow across all three optimises for none of them, and loses first deposits in the two markets it was not designed for.

---

## Evidence 3: Payments do not generalise

The deposit moment is where a regional template fails most expensively, because payment behaviour is local to the point of being municipal.

Payment-method breadth, held to the same operator across markets, tells the story. In Peru it fields twelve methods, the broadest in that cohort, spanning cards, e-wallets, QR, and bank rails. In Brazil it fields one: a single PIX entry point, against a competitor's eight bank-specific PIX variants and quick-select chips. Same operator. Same quarter. Twelve methods in one market, one in another, not because the operator got worse, but because Brazil's rail is PIX and the depth of a PIX implementation is now a competitive surface in its own right.

The deposit journey score follows the breadth. Where the payment set is thin and the local rail is under-built, the deposit touchpoint sits among the lowest in the market, and in a multi-homing region, a stalled deposit is not a retry. It is the next tab.

---

## Evidence 4: Responsible gambling is where the markets are actually converging, differently

If there is one journey where LATAM shows a shared direction, it is responsible gambling, but even here the placement, not the presence, is what separates operators, and it separates them by market.

The market's best-in-class pattern surfaces RG tools *inside* the flow: deposit limit, time-out, self-exclusion, and self-assessment presented on the registration confirmation screen, before the first deposit. In one market, a single operator does this and no competitor matches it. In another, the same operator drops the in-flow RG presence entirely and relegates it to a footer, while a rival makes limit-setting a mandatory step. In a third, the strongest RG posture in the cohort belongs to a different brand again, a dedicated limits screen plus a national self-exclusion register check at consent.

The signal for operators is that RG placement is becoming a trust-and-conversion surface, not only a compliance obligation, and the standard is being set market by market, by whichever operator moves first. An operator carrying a strong RG flow in one market and a footer link in the next is not inconsistent by accident. It is running a regional template in markets that have each moved past it.

---

## What this means commercially

The strategic error in Latin America is the org chart. A single regional owner, a single roadmap, and a single "LATAM launch" configuration will lose to local execution in every individual market, because the journeys that decide share are different in each.

**For product.** Localise the journeys that carry the deposit: onboarding to the national identity system, payments to the dominant local rail, RG to the market's emerging in-flow standard. These are not translation tasks. They are different products wearing the same brand.

**For commercial leadership.** Benchmark each market against its own competitive set, not against a regional average. A brand can lead Peru, sit mid-pack in Brazil, and edge Argentina in the same quarter. Averaging those three into a "LATAM position" hides exactly the gaps that are losing deposits.

**For expansion planning.** Treat each new LATAM market as a distinct build with a distinct competitive floor. The operators winning the region are not the ones with the best regional template. They are the ones with the best per-market execution, and the visibility to know, market by market, where they stand.

Latin America is not one opportunity. It is three (and counting), each with its own journey, its own rail, and its own competitive floor. The operators who see it that way will outbuild the ones still shipping a single continental playbook.

It is the region telling you it is not one region. The operators reading it market by market, with continuous benchmarking against each local set, are the ones building durable local advantage.*
