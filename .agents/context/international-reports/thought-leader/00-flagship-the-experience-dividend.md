# The Experience Dividend

### Across mature and emerging markets alike, the same three forces have collapsed product differentiation in iGaming. What remains is experience execution and promotional precision. The operators treating that as a measurable commercial system are pulling away. This is the throughline of a four-part regional study.

---

## The argument in one line

The games are the same, the odds are similar, and the platforms are shared. In every regulated market we benchmarked, the operator's decision to win or lose is made at the level of experience. Experience is now the last differentiator that is fully within an operator's control.

---

## The same three forces, everywhere

We studied four regions in depth: the United Kingdom, Brazil, the broader LATAM set of Brazil, Peru, and Argentina, and mature Europe across the Netherlands, France, Italy, Finland, Greece, and Norway. The markets could not be more different in age, regulation, or player behaviour. The forces acting on them are almost identical.

**Platforms have commoditised the product.** In the UK, the majority of regulated operators run on a small number of B2B stacks, sharing the same game library, the same payment infrastructure, and increasingly the same roadmap. In Brazil, a single sportsbook provider powers direct competitors, and a global group has deployed its proprietary engine into the market at a stated cost above $350m. When the engine is common, the engine cannot be the moat. Differentiation cannot wait on a vendor's release calendar, a constraint that now applies from São Paulo to Stoke.

**Margin is compressing through tax.** The UK's 40% Remote Gaming Duty has made every promotional pound cost materially more, and pushed Flutter, Betway, and Betfred to flag 20 to 25% budget cuts. Brazil is raising GGR tax on a published schedule and has passed a deposit-tax mechanism. Illinois runs a 40% rate in the US. Across Europe, regulatory constraints tighten the conditions under which operators can acquire and retain. The universal consequence is that you cannot outspend the market anymore. The margin for waste in the customer journey is contracting everywhere at once.

**Players multi-home by default.** Between 70% and 80% of UK players hold three to five operator accounts. The Brazilian bettor holds three to five. The US bettor holds three to five. The number is the same on three continents, and it changes the physics of the market. Loyalty is not lost loudly. Wallet share is reduced silently, across four apps at once. The 10% of players who drive 80% of revenue are the most likely to multi-home and the most sensitive to friction. One poor experience is not recovered from; the player simply uses the next tab.

Remove the two traditional levers, outspend and out-feature, and one lever remains. Experience. It is the only differentiator left that an operator fully controls, and it is measurable. That is the experience dividend. In a commoditised, tax-compressed, multi-homing market, the return on getting the journey right compounds, because everyone else has run out of other ways to compete.

---

## What the regional data shows

Each region expresses the same throughline in its own idiom. The four reports in this series develop them in full; here is the spine of each.

**United Kingdom, margin compression is a design brief.** The 40% duty changed what a promotion is *for*. Across 13,673 tracked promotional events and 21 brands, the operator running the most promotions in Britain holds one of the lowest shares of promotional voice, because its activity is thin and single-day. Meanwhile, one book roughly tripled its cost per boost across the spring while a rival matched its headline generosity at a third of the margin conceded, the difference between buying share and badging it. Volume stopped being a strategy. Precision became one. See *Report 01*.

**Brazil, regulation built the market and experience will decide it.** There are 17.7 million bettors, 78-plus licensed operators, shared engines, and common PIX rails. The scale leader sits mid-table on experience; a globally proven engine sits below three domestic-facing brands. Regulation put the hardest friction, KYC, at the highest-intent moment, the first deposit, and the operators that handle it best win the deposit in a market where every player has four alternatives open. With channelisation near half, better experience does not only move share between operators. It keeps players inside the regulated market at all. See *Report 02*.

**LATAM, there is no LATAM playbook.** Hold one operator constant and its scores do not travel: payments coverage from 90 in Peru to 50 in Brazil, help from 92 to 64, in the same quarter. Each market is built on a different national identity system (CPF; DNI; DNI plus CUIT), a different dominant rail, and a different competitive floor. A single regional template underperforms local execution in every individual market, because the journeys that decide share are different in each. See *Report 03*.

**Europe, the compliance floor is rising and differentiation moved upstairs.** Mature regulation standardised the basics: mandatory limits, digital identity, self-exclusion. That levelled the compliance floor and pushed the contest one storey up, to onboarding speed, payment breadth, casino discovery, and help. In France, the market leader scores 57 out of 100, a "Poor"-band score, first in a field where everyone is under-performing. Leading a poor market is a commercial risk disguised as an advantage. See *Report 04*.

Four regions. One pattern. The product has stopped separating operators, so the experience does.

---

## The three journeys where the dividend is won

Read across the regions, the friction concentrates in the same places regardless of jurisdiction. These are the surfaces where the experience dividend is actually paid.

**Onboarding and the first deposit.** This is the highest-intent, most fragile moment in every market, and regulation guarantees friction sits inside it. The winners integrate the national identity path (CPF pre-fill in Brazil, iDIN in the Netherlands, SPID in Italy, bank-ID in the Nordics) and complete verification in minutes. The laggards run manual flows that add days. Same regulation, opposite outcomes. The gap is execution, not compliance.

**The cashier.** Payment breadth does not generalise and does not converge. The same operator fields twelve methods in one market and one in another, three in a third and fifteen in a fourth. A thin cashier in a multi-homing market is a conversion tax at the highest-value step, paid every time a preferred method is missing and the deposit becomes the next tab. In a commoditised market, the cashier is one of the few surfaces that still visibly separates operators, and it is routinely under-built.

**Discovery and help.** Finding a game or a bet, and getting help when something breaks, are the journeys regulation does not prescribe and most operators under-invest in. They are also where retention is decided after acquisition is paid for. Across markets, the recurring gaps are the same: no header search, buried promotional terms, help off the persistent navigation, no documented complaints route. These are not design niceties. They are the leaks on acquisition an operator paid a rising CAC to buy.

## Why most operators cannot see it

If experience is the last differentiator, why is so much of it left on the table? Because most operators measure the wrong half of the market, and they measure it too slowly.

They measure their own output, not the market's intensity: event count, not share of voice; headline boost, not margin conceded. They review competitors quarterly in a market that rotates weekly. A count of 13,673 UK promotional events in 60 days is not a dataset any team can track by hand and still do its job. And they benchmark experience internally, against last quarter's version of themselves, rather than against the best execution across the competitive set. The result is a systematic blind spot: the illusion of market awareness between snapshots that are already out of date when they land.

The manual status quo of screenshots, weekly checks, internal threads, and an occasional agency audit was adequate when the market moved in quarters. It does not survive a market that moves in days, on three continents, with the margin for error contracting everywhere at once.

---

## What this means for 2026

The experience dividend is available in every market we studied, and it is being claimed by a minority. The operators pulling ahead share a single discipline: they treat experience and promotion as one measurable commercial system, benchmarked continuously against the market rather than reviewed periodically against themselves.

That discipline has three parts, and they are the same in every region.

- **Benchmark the market, not your output.** Share of voice over event count. Margin conceded over headline boost. The best execution anywhere over your own last version.
- **Measure the journeys that carry the money.** Onboarding, cashier, discovery, help, market by market, against the local competitive set rather than a regional average.
- **Do it continuously.** The market moves in days. Intelligence that arrives in quarters is a competitive blind spot, not a competitive advantage.

Product commoditised. Tax compressed the margin for waste. Players spread themselves across five accounts and defect on the first bad experience. In that market, experience is not a design concern. It is the last durable source of commercial advantage, and 2026 is the year the operators who measure it, and the operators who guess at it, separate for good.
